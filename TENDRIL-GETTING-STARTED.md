# Welcome to Tendril

> **Tendril is a personal stewardship system:** a place to notice what deserves attention, understand why it matters, and choose the next useful action.

Traditional task managers begin with lists of things to do. Tendril begins one level higher. It helps you tend the ongoing responsibilities, relationships, projects, and ideas that make those actions meaningful.

The goal is not to track everything. The goal is to build enough trusted context that Tendril can answer two practical questions:

- **What deserves my attention today?**
- **What may need renewed attention this week?**

---

## 1. The Tendril model

Tendril organizes your activity in layers. Not every item needs every layer, but the structure helps connect daily work to the bigger picture.

### Areas

Areas are broad parts of life that you intend to maintain over time.

Examples:

- Career
- Relationships
- Home
- Health
- Learning
- Community

Areas usually do not end. They provide a high-level view of where your attention is going.

### Roles

Roles describe who you are in a particular context.

Examples:

- Training and Development Specialist
- Team member
- Friend
- Mentor
- Learner
- Homeowner

A Role may contribute to more than one Area.

### Commitments

Commitments are ongoing responsibilities arising from your Areas, Roles, values, or priorities. Unlike a project or Action, a Commitment is not normally completed.

Examples:

- Maintain effective professional relationships
- Support stakeholder success
- Continue developing professionally
- Care for home systems
- Encourage and support others

Commitments help explain **why** related Threads and Actions matter.

### Threads

Threads are the central organizing concept in Tendril. A Thread is something that requires attention or stewardship over time.

Examples:

- A project
- A relationship
- An ongoing responsibility
- An idea being explored
- A learning interest

A Thread gathers the context needed to resume attention later, including its purpose, current state, related people, updates, and Actions. Threads may also contain a limited number of Subthreads when a meaningful distinction is needed.

### Relationships

Relationships are treated as a specialized form of stewardship rather than as a list of contacts.

A Relationship can include:

- The nature of the relationship
- Its current season
- A typical rhythm of interaction
- Meaningful interactions
- Notes and context
- Encouragement given or considered
- Related Threads and Actions

Rhythms are guides, not obligations. Tendril uses gentle prompts such as **Consider reconnecting** rather than treating a relationship as overdue.

### Actions

Actions are the concrete work you can perform and complete.

Examples:

- Draft a response
- Review feedback
- Schedule a conversation
- Enter time
- Send an encouraging message

Actions can be connected to Threads and Relationships so they retain their meaning. An Action may also be scheduled, assigned a due date, placed in focus, or marked Waiting or Blocked.

### Captures

A Capture is an incoming thought, request, obligation, note, or possibility that you have not yet clarified.

Capture is intentionally quick. You can decide later whether the item should become:

- An Action
- A Waiting item
- A new Thread
- A Thread update
- A Relationship entry
- A Reference
- An incubated possibility

One Capture may create several connected records. For example, an email may create an Action, add context to a Thread, and record a Relationship interaction.

## 2. Build your foundation

Do not try to model your entire life at once. Start with the parts that are active now and add context as it becomes useful.

### Step 1: Identify a few Commitments

Begin with three to seven ongoing responsibilities that currently shape your attention.

Use prompts such as:

- What must I continue caring for even when no deadline is present?
- What responsibilities arise from my work and personal Roles?
- What would become unhealthy or neglected if I stopped paying attention?
- What do I want to be more intentional about?

Write each Commitment as an ongoing responsibility, not as a goal or task.

Good:

- Maintain professional relationships
- Support reliable training operations
- Encourage the people around me

Less useful:

- Email Jordan
- Complete the course update
- Attend Friday's meeting

The less useful examples are Actions or project work that should connect to a larger Commitment.

### Step 2: Add the Relationships currently in view

Start with people connected to current work, active responsibilities, or relationships you deliberately want to tend. You do not need to import everyone you know.

For each Relationship, consider:

- How would I describe this relationship?
- What season is it currently in?
- Is there a natural rhythm of interaction?
- When was our last meaningful interaction?
- Is there anything useful to remember?
- Is there an opportunity for encouragement?

Choose rhythms realistically. A weekly rhythm may suit a close collaborator, while a quarterly or event-driven rhythm may suit a former colleague. More contact is not automatically better.

### Step 3: Create your active Threads

Create Threads for the projects, responsibilities, relationships, and ideas that are currently drawing attention.

For each Thread, record only enough to make re-entry easier:

- **Purpose:** Why does this Thread exist?
- **Desired outcome:** What would progress or completion look like?
- **Parent Thread:** Does it belong beneath another Thread?
- **Related person:** Who is closely connected to it?
- **Next review:** When should it return to your attention?
- **Resume note:** Where did you leave off, and what is likely next?

Avoid creating a deep hierarchy. If the structure becomes difficult to understand, simplify it.

### Step 4: Connect the layers

As the structure develops, connect Threads to the Commitments, Roles, and Areas they support.

A typical path might look like:

```text
Area: Career
  Role: Training and Development Specialist
    Commitment: Support stakeholder success
      Thread: Training intake redesign
        Action: Review stakeholder feedback
```

Not every Action needs the full path. Add only the context that helps you make decisions, resume work, or review your stewardship.

## 3. Begin using Tendril

### Capture first, clarify later

When something catches your attention, use **+ Capture** and record the shortest useful description.

Examples:

- Check whether the PCard course needs updating
- Follow up with Jordan about revised wording
- Idea for improving the Weekly Review

Add source details when they will help you return to an email, conversation, meeting, or webpage. Do not stop to classify everything unless doing so is genuinely easy.

### Clarify your Captures

Open **Capture** when you have time to decide what the items mean.

For each Capture, ask:

1. Does this require attention?
2. Is there concrete work for me to do?
3. Does it add context to an existing Thread or Relationship?
4. Am I waiting for someone or something?
5. Is this a new Thread, a Reference, or something to reconsider later?

If work must be performed and completed, create an **Action**. Notes, interactions, and updates provide context but do not replace Actions.

### Build current Actions

Start with Actions that are already active, due soon, or occupying mental space. Connect them to the relevant Threads and Relationships when doing so adds useful context.

Use dates intentionally:

- **Scheduled date:** When you intend to work on it
- **Due date:** When it actually needs to be completed
- **Poke date:** When a Waiting item should be reconsidered or followed up

Use **Focus** for the small set of Actions that deserves attention now. Focus is not another priority level. It is a temporary choice about where to engage.

### Use Waiting without losing visibility

When progress depends on someone or something else, mark the Action **Waiting** and record:

- What or whom you are waiting on
- When a response is expected
- When Tendril should surface the item for a possible follow-up

A follow-up Action can be completed while the original Waiting item remains open.

## 4. Use the main views

### Today

Use **Today** as the practical starting point for the day. It surfaces Focus Actions, Capture counts, Waiting items, and active Threads.

### Capture

Use **Capture** to review unprocessed inputs and turn them into trusted records. The Capture queue should be cleared regularly, but it does not need to be empty at all times.

### Threads

Use **Threads** to browse what you are stewarding, update purpose and resume notes, and notice Threads that lack a next Action.

### Actions

Use **Actions** to manage concrete work. Filter and sort the list to focus on open work, completed history, due dates, Threads, or Relationships.

### Relationships

Use **Relationships** to review rhythms, recent entries, open linked Actions, and opportunities to reconnect or encourage.

### Operations

Use **Operations** to find what may be slipping:

- Overdue Actions
- Blocked work
- Waiting items ready for a poke
- Threads without an open Action

This is the task-oriented safety net beneath Tendril's stewardship model.

### Reviews

Use **Reviews** to step back from individual items and make deliberate choices about attention.

## 5. Establish a review rhythm

### Daily Review

Use the Daily Review to become ready for the day, not to reorganize the entire system.

A useful Daily Review should help you:

1. Notice unprocessed Captures without becoming distracted by them
2. Check operational risks
3. Select a small set of Focus Actions
4. Leave with a clear starting point

Three Focus Actions is often enough. Five should normally be the upper limit.

### Weekly Stewardship Review

Use the Weekly Stewardship Review to examine the larger system.

A useful Weekly Review should help you:

1. See outstanding work and operational risks
2. Select Threads that deserve attention
3. Notice Relationships approaching or beyond their typical rhythm
4. Identify encouragement opportunities
5. Create or adjust Actions where needed
6. Prepare a practical snapshot for the coming week

The purpose is not to produce a perfect plan. The purpose is to renew awareness and make intentional choices.

---

## 6. A simple first-week approach

For the first week, keep the setup small.

### Before beginning

- Identify three to seven Commitments
- Add five to fifteen currently relevant Relationships
- Create five to ten active Threads
- Add the Actions already needing attention

### Each day

- Capture incoming concerns quickly
- Clarify Captures at a convenient time
- Run the Daily Review
- Work from a small Focus list
- Leave resume notes when stopping mid-Thread

### At the end of the week

- Run the Weekly Stewardship Review
- Review Waiting and Blocked Actions
- Select next week's Focus Threads and Relationships
- Notice at least one encouragement opportunity
- Export a backup

After the week, adjust the system based on friction you actually experienced rather than trying to anticipate every possible need.

---

## 7. Principles to remember

- **Stewardship before productivity:** Tend what matters rather than merely accumulating completed tasks.
- **Context before categorization:** Add structure when it helps you understand or resume attention.
- **Capture without friction:** Recording something should be easier than continuing to hold it mentally.
- **Actions represent work:** If you must do and complete something, track it as an Action.
- **Rhythms are guides:** Relationships and ongoing responsibilities do not all demand the same frequency.
- **Reviews create trust:** Tendril becomes useful when it regularly brings the right concerns back into view.
- **Gentle does not mean vague:** Operations provides a clear view of what is overdue, blocked, waiting, or disconnected.
- **Start small:** Let the system grow from use.

---

## The shortest version

If you remember only one workflow, use this one:

```text
Capture what has your attention
  → Clarify what it means
    → Connect it to the right Thread or Relationship
      → Create the next useful Action
        → Review daily and weekly
          → Tend what matters
```
